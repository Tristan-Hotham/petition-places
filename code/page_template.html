    Any UK resident or British citizen can start or sign a petition to Parliament. When you sign, the site asks for your postcode, and it publishes a running count of signatures in every constituency. That makes each petition a little map of who cared. I took the {{sample.petitions|int}} petitions of the current Parliament that had reached about 3,000 signatures by the time I collected the data, {{inputs.detail_signatures|int}} signatures between them, and asked a simple question: what does each constituency sign more than the rest of the country does?

    01 · Every petition has a mapSome are everywhere, some are somewhere

    To compare petitions of very different sizes, I measure each seat against itself. A seat that gives a petition the same share of its signatures as the country as a whole scores one. Twice the usual share scores two. Some seats sign far more petitions than others overall, from {{activity.min_signatures|int}} signatures in {{activity.min_signatures_seat}} to {{activity.max_signatures|int}} in {{activity.max_signatures_seat}}, so this keeps the busiest seats from dominating every map. Small counts are pulled gently towards one, so a seat with a handful of signatures cannot top a list by chance.

    The biggest petition, "{{cases.700143.a}}", with {{cases.700143.n|int}} signatures, is close to the national pattern everywhere: only {{cases.700143.D_pc|f0}}% of its signatures would have to move for it to match the overall map exactly. At the other end, "{{cases.711355.a}}" drew {{cases.711355.top.0.lq|f0}} times the usual share in {{cases.711355.top.0.seat}} and {{cases.711355.top.1.lq|f0}} times in {{cases.711355.top.1.seat}}, and {{cases.711355.D_pc|f0}}% of its signatures would have to move. Between the two sit petitions with clear but broad geographies: "{{cases.700138.a}}" is strongest in {{cases.700138.top.1.seat}} and {{cases.700138.top.2.seat}}; "{{cases.700036.a}}" in {{cases.700036.top.0.seat}} and {{cases.700036.top.1.seat}}, both on the edge of the Peak District.

    Six petitions on the same scale. Red: more than the seat's usual share; blue: less.

    Every one of the {{H1.n|int}} maps is clustered: neighbouring seats sign alike far more than chance would give. The typical petition has a spatial correlation (Moran's I) of {{H1.median_I|f2}}; shuffle the seats at random and it falls to {{P1.moran_placebo|f2}}.

    02 · Local causesThe most lopsided maps

    Ten petitions are about a particular part of the UK, among them Cornwall's status, two about the rail link between Oxford and Cambridge, the Scottish Parliament, Northern Ireland veterans, the London Mayor. Their maps are far more lopsided than any other topic's. In the typical local petition {{H2.D_local_pc|f0}}% of signatures would have to move to match the national pattern, against {{H2.D_national_pc|f0}}% for all other petitions, and {{H2.top10_local_pc|f0}}% of its signatures come from just ten seats. Allowing for petition size, local causes are {{H2.local.pc|f0}}% more uneven than national ones (95% interval {{H2.local.lo|f0}}% to {{H2.local.hi|f0}}%). "{{cases.762755.a}}" was signed {{cases.762755.top.0.lq|f0}} times as heavily as usual in {{cases.762755.top.0.seat}}.

    Among national topics, immigration petitions have the most uneven maps (a typical {{H2.fam.immigration.median_D_pc|f0}}% would have to move) and petitions about elections and democracy the least ({{H2.fam.democracy.median_D_pc|f0}}%). Immigration petitions are uneven partly because they pull in opposite directions: in an exploratory split (section 04), petitions asking to keep or open routes to settlement were signed most in more diverse seats, and petitions asking for tighter rules most in whiter seats with a higher Reform UK vote.

    03 · Signature personalitiesTwo ways seats differ

    Put all {{sample.petitions|int}} maps together and ask what they share. The first summary dimension (a principal component) holds {{H3.pc1_pc|f0}}% of the variation between seats, the second {{H3.pc2_pc|f0}}%. The first separates seats with more graduates, younger people and more ethnic diversity (correlation with the share holding a degree {{H3.corr.PC1.degree|f2}}) from older and whiter seats with a higher Reform UK vote in 2024 ({{H3.corr.PC1.ruk|f2}}). The second separates seats held by Labour with poorer health from Conservative and Liberal Democrat seats with older populations. Neither is a party line: they are bundles of topics that tend to travel together.

    Seat scores on the two main personalities.

    These personalities are stable. Split the petitions into the {{H3.n_early|int}} created first and the {{H3.n_late|int}} created later, and the seats line up almost identically: correlation {{H3.stab1|f2}} for the first personality and {{H3.stab2|f2}} for the second. Grouping seats by their whole profile gives {{H3.k|int}} groups: {{H3.clusters.Towns, suburbs and countryside.n|int}} seats of towns, suburbs and countryside, {{H3.clusters.Diverse cities.n|int}} seats in diverse cities ({{H3.clusters.Diverse cities.london|int}} in London), and {{H3.clusters.Scotland and Northern Ireland.n|int}} seats, almost all in Scotland and Northern Ireland, which probably partly reflects the many petitions about English schools and services that have little to do with them.

    04 · Does it follow the vote?Partly, and the census more

    For each petition I asked how much of its seat map the 2024 general election vote shares can account for. The typical answer is {{H4a.median_r2_votes_pc|f0}}% in Great Britain, about as much as knowing the region alone ({{H4a.median_r2_region_pc|f0}}%). In England and Wales, four census shares (age, degrees, health and ethnicity) account for more ({{H4a.median_r2_census_ew_pc|f0}}%) than the vote ({{H4a.median_r2_votes_ew_pc|f0}}%). The biggest petition, "{{cases.700143.a}}", follows it closely: the vote accounts for {{cases.700143.r2_votes_pc|f0}}% of its map, which correlates {{cases.700143.best_r|f2}} with the Conservative share. Others hardly follow it at all, such as "{{H4a.low_r2.0.a}}" ({{H4a.low_r2.0.r2|pcx}}).

    Two planned tests looked at direction. Petitions about Brexit and the EU were signed less where Reform UK did well ({{H4b.eu.ruk_pct.pc|f1}}% per standard deviation of the Reform UK share, 95% interval {{H4b.eu.ruk_pct.lo|f1}}% to {{H4b.eu.ruk_pct.hi|f1}}%), even after age, degrees, health, ethnicity and region, and that held in all {{robust.eu_ruk.n_checks|int}} checks. They were signed more where the Greens did well ({{H4b.eu.green_pct.pc|f1}}%, held in {{robust.eu_green.n_sig|int}} of {{robust.eu_green.n_checks|int}} checks) and the Liberal Democrats did well ({{H4b.eu.ld_pct.pc|f1}}%, held in {{robust.eu_ld.n_sig|int}}). Immigration petitions as a whole were signed slightly more where Reform UK did well ({{H4b.immigration.ruk_pct.pc|f1}}%, {{H4b.immigration.ruk_pct.lo|f1}}% to {{H4b.immigration.ruk_pct.hi|f1}}%), but that held in only {{robust.immigration_ruk.n_sig|int}} of {{robust.immigration_ruk.n_checks|int}} checks, so I do not treat it as solid. Splitting immigration petitions by what they ask for, which I did only after seeing that result, suggests why: those asking for tighter rules track the Reform UK vote (map correlation {{exploratory.restrict.corr_ruk|f2}}), while those asking to keep or open routes run the other way ({{exploratory.open.corr_ruk|f2}}).

    05 · ChecksIs it real?

    Repeat petitions on the same subject, such as the {{P2.n_groups|int}} groups of near-identical petitions on a general election, the personal allowance or rejoining the EU, should have similar maps if the maps are real and not noise. They do: the typical correlation between two repeat petitions' maps is {{P2.median_within|f2}}, against {{P2.median_random|f2}} for random pairs on different topics. The clustering result holds with other neighbour definitions, other denominators and raw counts. The most over-signed petition in each seat is the same with or without the gentle pull towards one in {{top.same_as_raw_pc|f0}}% of seats.

    06 · How I did thisCounts, shares and a written plan

    Petitions. The petitions site publishes every petition of the current Parliament ({{inputs.all_petitions|int}} by the time I collected them, {{inputs.states.rejected|int}} of them rejected) and, for each, signatures by constituency. I used the {{sample.petitions|int}} that had about 3,000 or more signatures, which hold {{inputs.detail_share_pc|f0}}% of all signatures. I sorted them into topics with keyword rules and then read every one, moving {{inputs.n_overrides|int}} by hand.

    Measures. Each seat's share of a petition's signatures is compared with its share of all petition signatures. The 2023 boundary review gave nearly every seat an electorate within 5% of the same size, so raw counts are close to rates. I wrote the analysis plan down before computing any of these measures, and anything added later is labelled as exploratory.

    What this cannot say. A seat is not a person: a petition signed heavily in a seat with a high Reform UK vote does not mean Reform UK voters signed it. Signing is a minority activity, people may give a postcode that is not where they live, and campaigns can push a petition hard in one place. Petitions still open were still collecting signatures. Northern Ireland and Scotland have no census shares in the data I used, and the vote tests leave out Northern Ireland, where different parties stand.

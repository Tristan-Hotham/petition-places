Every time you sign a UK Parliament petition, you give a postcode, and the site counts your signature in your constituency. That quietly turns every petition into a map. I put {{sample.petitions|int}} of those maps side by side to see what each part of the country signs more than the rest.

The petitions site is a busy door into Parliament. Since the 2024 election people have started {{inputs.all_petitions|int}} petitions, and {{inputs.states.rejected|int}} of them were rejected before they ever went live. Petitions that reach 10,000 signatures get a government response, and those that reach 100,000 are considered for a debate. They cover everything: fireworks, farm taxes, rare diseases, school attendance fines, rail lines, Gaza, the personal allowance, dogs in laboratories.

I took the {{sample.petitions|int}} petitions that had passed about 3,000 signatures, {{inputs.detail_signatures|int}} signatures between them, and for every one of the 650 constituencies asked a simple question. Of all the petition signatures from this seat, what share went to this petition, and is that more or less than the country as a whole? A seat that signs a petition at twice its usual rate scores two. Measuring each seat against itself matters, because some seats sign far more petitions than others: from {{activity.min_signatures|int}} signatures in {{activity.min_signatures_seat}} to {{activity.max_signatures|int}} in {{activity.max_signatures_seat}}.

In between are petitions with clear but broad patterns. "{{cases.700138.a}}" was strongest in {{cases.700138.top.1.seat}} and {{cases.700138.top.2.seat}}. Local causes are the most lopsided

Only ten petitions are about one particular part of the UK, among them Cornwall, the Scottish Parliament, the London Mayor, Northern Ireland veterans, the A1 in the North of England and the rail link between Oxford and Cambridge. Their maps are far more uneven than any other topic's. In the typical one, {{H2.D_local_pc|f0}}% of signatures would have to move to match the national pattern, against {{H2.D_national_pc|f0}}% for everything else, and {{H2.top10_local_pc|f0}}% of its signatures come from just ten seats. Two signature personalities

Put all the maps together and a fairly simple structure appears. Two summary dimensions hold {{H3.pc12_pc|f0}}% of all the variation between seats. The first separates seats with more graduates, younger people and more ethnic diversity from older, whiter seats where Reform UK did better in 2024. The second separates Labour-held seats with poorer health from Conservative and Liberal Democrat seats with older populations, and it is marked by petitions about nature, the countryside and animal welfare.

{{IMG|b_pca.png|Two hexagon maps of the main signature personalities: the first red in London and big cities, the second red in Scotland, Northern Ireland and northern English cities|Seat scores on the two main signature personalities.}}

That stability is the finding I like best. These are not the traces of one or two big campaigns: the same pattern appears in the petitions started early in the Parliament and the ones started later. Grouping seats by their whole profile gives three kinds of place: {{H3.clusters.Towns, suburbs and countryside.n|int}} seats of towns, suburbs and countryside, {{H3.clusters.Diverse cities.n|int}} diverse city seats, {{H3.clusters.Diverse cities.london|int}} of them in London, and {{H3.clusters.Scotland and Northern Ireland.n|int}} seats, almost all in Scotland and Northern Ireland, which probably partly reflects how many petitions are about schools and services in England.

{{IMG|b_clusters.png|Hexagon map of three groups of seats: gold for most of England and Wales, red for London and other cities, blue for Scotland and Northern Ireland|Three kinds of seat, grouped by their whole petition profile.}}

Does it follow the vote?

Partly. For the typical petition, the 2024 vote shares account for {{H4a.median_r2_votes_pc|f0}}% of its map, about the same as simply knowing the region. In England and Wales, four census measures (age, degrees, health and ethnicity) account for more ({{H4a.median_r2_census_ew_pc|f0}}%) than the vote ({{H4a.median_r2_votes_ew_pc|f0}}%). Most petitions, about animals, health or money, are signed right across the political map.

Two links were in my plan from the start. Petitions about the EU were signed less where Reform UK did well, even after allowing for age, degrees and region, and that held in every one of {{robust.eu_ruk.n_checks|int}} checks. Immigration petitions as a group were signed a little more where Reform UK did well, but that held in only {{robust.immigration_ruk.n_sig|int}} of the checks. When I looked at the petitions themselves, a likely reason stood out: the group mixes petitions for tighter rules with petitions from people asking to keep their route to settlement, and the two have nearly opposite maps.

How I did this

The petitions site publishes signatures by constituency for every petition. I used the {{sample.petitions|int}} with about 3,000 or more signatures, which hold {{inputs.detail_share_pc|f0}}% of all signatures. Seats with only a few signatures for a petition are pulled gently towards the usual rate, so no seat tops a list by chance. I sorted the petitions into topics with keyword rules and then read every one, moving {{inputs.n_overrides|int}} by hand. Votes and census shares for the 2024 seats come from the voteReproject package, and the hexagon layout from Open Innovations. I wrote the analysis plan down before computing any of the measures, and the paper reports it as planned, with later additions marked.

What this cannot tell you: who signed. A petition signed heavily in a seat with a high vote for one party does not mean that party's voters signed it. People who sign petitions are not typical of everyone, postcodes are self-reported, and a determined local campaign can fill a map on its own. These maps describe places, not people.
